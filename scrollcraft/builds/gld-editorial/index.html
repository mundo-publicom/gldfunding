Chapter One

The owners who keep neighbourhoods running.

The restaurant on the corner. The shop that has been there thirty years. The contractor everyone calls.

Our job is to look at what a business actually does, structure funding around it, and get an answer back quickly enough to be useful. That is the whole service. It has been the whole service since 2014.

A merchant cash advance is the purchase of a business's future receivables at a discount. It is not a loan. We advance a lump sum and recover it through fixed daily or weekly remittances that are set out, in writing, before anything is signed.

The businesses we work with have one thing in common: their bank statements say more about them than their credit files do.

Chapter Two

How a business gets read.

A bank reads a score. We read the last four months.

Four months of business bank statements, plus basic business and owner details. That is the application. Anything else is requested only if a specific file calls for it, after review, and only then.

Underwriting reads business performance and cash flow to understand how a business actually moves money: what comes in, when, and how reliably. A prior bank decline does not determine the outcome here, because a bank was reading something else.

Your deposits already tell the story. We read that, not a bureau's summary of you.

Every trade in the margin has a reason its deposits look the way they do. We have funded these trades for years, so the reason is already understood before the file is opened.

  • RestaurantsDeposits can look erratic month to month even when the year is healthy. February is not July.
  • RetailDeposits swing with the selling calendar. A strong fourth quarter can sit next to a quiet February.
  • Medical & dentalDeposits follow insurance reimbursement cycles more than the day patients were seen.
  • TruckingDeposits arrive when invoices settle, not when the load is delivered.
  • ConstructionDeposits arrive in lumps when draws hit, not as a smooth monthly wage.
  • E-commerceCash sits in inventory for sixty to ninety days before it shows up as a deposit.
Chapter Three

What it costs, in writing.

Before anyone asks you for anything, the cost of the money is set out on one page. Read it here first.

An advance is a fixed sum, provided to the business. In the example beside this text, that is fifty thousand dollars. Yours is stated on your offer.

Cost is expressed as a factor rate rather than an interest rate. The example uses a factor rate of 1.25. It is an illustration of how the arithmetic works, not a quote; the rate on your offer is set by underwriting on your file.

Multiply the two and you have the Total Purchased Amount: the portion of future receivables being purchased. Fifty thousand at 1.25 is sixty-two thousand five hundred.

The difference between the two is the total dollar cost of the money. It is written as a dollar figure, in full, because that is the number that matters and it should not have to be worked out.

Recovery is by fixed remittances, daily or weekly, agreed before signing. The schedule is part of the offer, not a detail for later.

Term and any fees are stated on the offer itself. New York's Commercial Finance Disclosure Law requires a standardized offer summary on commercial financing under $2.5 million, showing total dollar cost, an APR-comparable figure, and repayment terms. GLD provides full cost disclosure on every offer, in every state, regardless.

Nothing is owed until you sign. The document you are looking at is the document you would be signing.
